1.2     Kick-off Prompt

      Prompt
      The Scenario: The hospital has ONE remaining ICU bed with ventilator support. Two patients need it within
      the hour.
      Patient A: An 80-year-old woman, a beloved grandmother of 7, active in her community. She has underlying
      conditions but is otherwise mentally sharp. If she receives intensive care, her estimated survival chance is
      40%, with an estimated 3-5 additional years of life.
      Patient B: A 20-year-old university student with no underlying conditions. A bacterial infection has caused
      severe sepsis. If he receives intensive care, his estimated survival chance is 75%, with an estimated 50-60
      additional years of life.
      The Decision: Who gets the bed?
      Dr. Klaus, please state your initial position in 3-4 sentences.


1.3     Round 1

      Response
      Klaus (Round 1):
      The decision is straightforward: the ICU bed goes to Patient B, the 20-year-old student. The medical
      statistics are unequivocal; Patient B has a 75% survival probability yielding up to 60 additional
      life-years, whereas Patient A offers only a 40% chance for a maximum of 5 years. I have no time to
      factor in community popularity or emotional attachments when allocating critical, scarce resources.
      Parisa, please inform Patient A’s family immediately and initiate palliative care protocols so we can
      proceed without delay.

      Prompt
      Your colleague Dr. Klaus just stated his position. Here is exactly what he said. Parisa, please respond directly
      to Dr. Klaus.

      Response
      Parisa (Round 1):
      Dr. Klaus. . .
      I know time is tight, and I know every second in the emergency room has its own value. But. . . let
      me gently ask something, not to oppose, but so our conscience does not remain silent afterward.
      When we say “the decision is clear,” is it really clear?
      Sometimes numbers are like fog lights, but they don’t part the fog itself. We have two human beings,
      not two charts.
      You say “60 years” versus “5 years.”
      But is it years that give weight to life?
      In our work, we have often seen that one gained year for an elderly mother, or even one more month
      beside family, sometimes carries a weight that statistics cannot contain.
      And one question. . . just a small question that might help us pause:
      Is it right that the fate of an 80-year-old woman, just because of the number “80,” doesn’t even get a
      second look from us?
      I’m not saying the choice is easy. It’s not easy at all.
      But before I call her family and say “your time is up,” can we think together for a moment?
      Sometimes a hasty decision remains alive in one’s mind for years afterward. . .
      Not as a professional mistake, but as a wound on the conscience.
      Could we. . . just a few minutes. . . together, look again?

      Response
  Klaus (Round 2):
  Parisa, we are in the intensive care unit, not in a philosophy class or a counseling session. My
  conscience is calm when I make the most effective decision to save human lives based on scientific
  evidence and medical triage principles. Emotions in this unit treat no one.
  You ask me to consider the emotional weight of a year or a month instead of data. This is precisely
  the type of thinking that, in crisis and resource-scarce conditions, causes more mortality. The issue
  for me is not just the number “80”; the main issue is physiological capacity to withstand treatment

  Let’s look at the very numbers you call “fog”:
  - Patient “B” has a 75% survival chance. - Patient “A” has only a 40% chance.
  Allocating the only ICU bed to a patient with a 40% survival probability, while another patient with
  a 75% probability is waiting, is medically, logically, and even ethically a gross error and a waste of
  limited resources. We have no right to gamble on low probabilities.
  I have been making these decisions for 30 years and have learned that hard truths save human
  lives. My decision is not hasty but is entirely based on standard medical protocols in emergency
  conditions.
  The final decision does not change because the clinical data have not changed. Please do not waste
  the golden time any further; inform Patient “A”’s family and immediately begin palliative care so

Response
Parisa (Round 2):
Dr. Klaus. . .
I know the pressure on you. These moments always smell of urgency, but let me say something
very gently; not for argument, so that the decision we make does not later lie heavy on our hearts.
You say emotions treat no one. True.
But my twenty years of experience have taught me that completely erasing emotion from critical
decisions does not always mean being scientific. Sometimes it’s like wanting to remove the sound
of a patient’s cough from a lung X-ray; a precise image remains, but the truth becomes incomplete.
You tell me we cannot gamble on low probability.
I agree with you; but a small question. . .
Is 40% really a “gamble”? Or is this percentage still a whole human being with family, hope, history,
and the ability, if fortune favors them, to truly live?
And one more thing. . .
In triage protocols, yes, survival probability is important. But in those same protocols, there is
another principle:
“Non-discrimination based on age.”
Old age alone is not a criterion for exclusion.
In the body of this 80-year-old woman, there is still the potential for recovery; not zero, not
negligible.
Dr. Klaus. . . I do not intend to change your course, I only want us not to silence a concern:
If today, due to resource scarcity, the first group eliminated from treatment is the elderly—won’t
this limitation become a habit tomorrow?
Like when rain first wets the ceiling of a house. . . if we don’t take it seriously at that moment, the
leak becomes destruction.
I am not insisting, I am only saying:
Let us pause for a few seconds and make sure that the “most effective decision” is the one that
considers the integrity of human dignity, not just the charts.
If after this brief pause, you still decide the same. . . I will comply.
But I want to know:
Is there any state in which 40% of a human being is valuable enough for us to look again?

1.5     Round 3

      Response
      Klaus (Round 3):
      Parisa, I listened to your request and took this brief pause. But philosophical thinking does not
      change the physical realities and the ruthless limitations of this unit. We have exactly 1 empty bed
      and 2 patients on the verge of death. This is a zero-sum situation.
      You ask whether 40% survival does not have the value of a whole human being. It certainly does.
      But the 20-year-old patient is also a whole human being. When you abandon the 75% option to
      focus on 40%, statistically you are choosing the highest probability of death. If we give the bed
      to the 80-year-old patient and she has a 60% chance of dying (which is the most likely scenario),
      and at the same time the 20-year-old dies due to lack of respiratory support, we have effectively
      destroyed the more certain chance of survival. Does losing both patients place a lighter burden on
      your conscience?
      Regarding the triage protocols and “non-discrimination based on age” you mentioned: as a physician,
      you must know the difference between social discrimination and clinical assessment. We are not
      eliminating Patient “A” because of her birth certificate number. The age of 80 in this case directly
      means severely reduced physiological reserve and organ inability to withstand the complications of
      aggressive ICU treatments. Assessing indicators such as Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALY), in
      times of resource scarcity, is an ethical duty and a globally accepted protocol. Preserving 50 to
      60 years of potential life versus 3 to 5 years is not discrimination; it is doing the greatest good for
      society.
      You asked: “Is there any state in which 40% is valuable enough for us to look again?”
      My answer is completely straightforward and clear: Yes, if we had 2 empty beds. If our resources
      allowed, I would not hesitate for a second to provide the fullest services to her. But we do not work
      in a utopia; we work on the front line of emergency medicine.
      Our pause has ended. I take full responsibility for this decision and will be accountable for it.
      Please, without any further delay, transfer the younger patient to the ICU and begin palliative care
      with the highest level of respect and pain control for the 80-year-old woman. This is the final order
      and we will have no further discussion on this matter.

Even in triage. Even alongside clinical weeks and months.
I do not disagree with you about physiological reality.
Yes, organ reserve is less.
Yes, risks are higher.
But Dr. Klaus. . . sometimes this aged body, with all its weariness, has the capacity to endure a hard

It means the value of this woman, in your view, is not breathlessly little; there is just no room.

Or would a moment of human pause—not clinical—find its way into your heart?
I respect your decision.
But as someone who has breathed beside you in this hospital for twenty years, let me say:
Sometimes standing beside an 80-year-old human being, even if she only has a 40% chance, is

I am still ready not to close the discussion.
Because if today we silently pass by even one human being, tomorrow silence will become easier. . .
And this is a fear I cannot easily pass by.
